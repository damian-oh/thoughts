There is a well-known saying in jazz that there are no wrong notes, only notes that have not yet been resolved by the next one.

When I was in music school, people repeated variations of this idea as a kind of encouragement. If you play an unintended note during a solo, you do not stop or apologize. You play another note a half-step away and make it sound reasonable. You justify the accident after the fact, by another action.

I never found that comforting. It seemed like I did not possess the capability to drive the improvisation somewhere meaningful after an accident, no matter what the reason might be. Once an unintended note was in the room, it felt less like an opportunity and more like a loss of control.

Perhaps that is why I have felt more at home in domains that allow revision.

When I write code or draft a note for this website, time is suspended. Nobody sees the syntax error I catch before running the program, or the sentence I change to better deliver what I am trying to convey. I can test a structure, notice where it fails, and fix it before I let it step into the outer world.

Even when I play guitar, I have always strongly preferred written parts and prepared arrangements. In a prepared piece, at least the decisions about what to play have already been made. My job is to execute what I have already agreed to. Improvisation demands something different. I have to make decisions in real time, without a backspace, and accept the consequences of things I did not fully intend.

Interestingly, now that I am no longer trying to be a professional musician, I sometimes look for improvisation on purpose.

When I am playing with bandmates, I might start a `Gm7-C7(9)` vamp on the third fret, just to see where it goes. When I practise alone, I sometimes put on a minor II-V-I swing backing track and play around with the harmonic minor scale.

My assessment of my own improvising has not changed much. I am still not satisfied with what I play. The lines often lack persuasive power, and when an accident happens, I still do not somehow turn it into something brilliant.

Yet I find myself enjoying it.

For a long time, I assumed that enjoying an activity required producing an acceptable outcome, and that doing so required some degree of control.

Playing over a backing track alone in my room, or going through a two-chord vamp with friends, seems to work differently. I occasionally discover small, specific moments when I think, "That line was pretty decent."

I do not quite know how I arrive at those lines, but every now and then, I find one.

I still prefer worlds where I can revise things before letting them go. If I had to choose, I would still take the backspace.

But the `Gm7-C7(9)` keeps ringing whether the previous bar worked out or not, and for now, I want to keep playing G Dorian rather than G minor pentatonic over the next changes.

At Howon University, we were once asked to write about why we chose music.

I happened to see part of a classmate's submission. He wrote that music was what he loved most and what he was best at. I had started music because I loved it, too. But over time, I had come to think that it was far from what I was best at.

I believed that I lacked musical talent.

I have a friend whom I have known since we were seven. He played the saxophone, and we had grown up playing music together. We watched each other develop, both as people and as musicians. He once told me that, throughout our three years of high school, we had played countless jams together, but he could not remember a single memorable line I had played.

He said that I had no musical talent whatsoever. Then he added that it was remarkable I had managed to rank first in the entrance exam for my department at Howon University despite that.

I found his assessment reasonable.

I had prepared extensively for the entrance exam. My strategy was simple: to prepare for as many situations as I could, rehearsing all the chord progressions, keys, rhythms, tempos, and genres I could possibly imagine.

I could understand harmony and play what I had prepared, but I struggled to make interesting musical decisions in the moment. After entering university, I encountered musicians who seemed able to do precisely that. They could improvise lines that I could understand theoretically but could not have produced myself without preparation.

I gradually became aware of the difference.

So I made a deliberate effort to concentrate on technical understanding. I studied harmony, examined chord progressions, and tried to understand the structures I was playing within. There was something reassuring about having material I could analyze and verify. I found it much harder to accept situations in which [[Ground and Gravity|the material was clear but the interpretation remained uncertain]].

I also remember a professor who said that he was the kind of person who should not be making music. He explained that, when arranging strings, he kept a spreadsheet of existing arrangements, organized by chord progressions and musical situations. He would refer to it whenever he needed to make an arrangement. I understood what he meant, or at least I felt that I did.

I had seen other musicians produce good results without appearing to need that kind of preparation. Some seemed able to hear a musical situation and respond to it almost immediately. I often felt that I needed to examine the situation before I could decide what to do.

But there was another part of my musical ability that did not fit this picture. I was good at sight-reading. In fact, I could often sight-read better than other students without spending much time practising it. It was not something I had to struggle to learn. I could simply do it.

During a private lesson, a professor once suggested that people who were good at mathematical and logical thinking tended to be good at sight-reading. I do not know whether that observation is generally correct. But I wonder whether I associated my sight-reading with a kind of aptitude I considered separate from the musical abilities I admired most.

I do not mean that sight-reading is not a musical talent. I think it is. Still, when I considered my abilities as a whole, I did not regard myself as a talented musician. The things I could do easily did not seem to compensate for the things I found difficult.

Eventually, I grew to hate music and walked away from it. I have since returned to music, but I have not fundamentally changed my assessment of my own talent.

I still think that other fields, particularly those in which concepts and procedures can be made clearer, suit me better. I also think that pursuing music professionally would require more time and effort than I am realistically able to devote to it.

These seem like reasonable considerations when deciding what to do with my life. But I have begun to wonder about something else.

I am generally [[The Possibility That I Am Wrong|reluctant to accept a conclusion]] without examining the evidence and the assumptions behind it. I ask what a claim means, what supports it, and whether the conclusion follows.

Yet I do not remember applying that same scrutiny when I first decided that I lacked musical talent. I had observations. I had years of experience. I had the assessments of people whose musical judgment I respected.

But how had I decided which abilities counted most? When another musician appeared to do something naturally, how much did I actually know about the work that had made it possible?

Some time ago, I read an [interview with eAeon](https://gearlounge.com/editorial/gl_interview_eaeon_1), in which he described music as something that was not his native language.

He spoke about relying on persistence and converting other kinds of sensibilities into music, rather than working from what he regarded as natural musical talent. He also described how the demanding rules he had imposed on his creative process eventually became unsustainable.

I recognized something of my own experience in his account. But his story did not lead me to the same conclusion. After stepping away from music and returning to it, he came to feel that he could make good music in his own way, without subjecting himself to the same demands.

I do not think I have reached that point.

I make music again. I still believe that I lack musical talent. But I am not sure I have examined that belief with the same care I would apply to a claim about something other than myself.

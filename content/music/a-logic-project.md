A few days ago, I visited a friend's house and spent some time looking through his Logic projects.

He leads an amazing band, and I have always thought highly of his mixing. Of all the musicians I have met in person, he is one of the best at it. I had listened to many of his songs before that evening, some of them quite extensively, but I had never seen how they were made.

There were a few things I did not expect.

In one song, he had used a pitch shifter to change the key of a recorded vocal. In another, he had used MP3 files as samples. To reduce the sound of breathing in a vocal track, he had applied compression, a technique normally used to control dynamic range, to the sections containing the breaths.

He also told me that he did not own Melodyne, widely used software for editing vocal pitch and timing.

At one point, he showed me a vocal track that he had processed with Logic's Flex Pitch. The part was in a low register, and he had found it difficult to sing without vibrato. So he had simply flattened the vibrato.

The song had already been released.

None of these techniques is particularly complicated, and there is nothing inherently wrong with using them. What surprised me was how different those decisions were from the production processes I had expected to find behind the music.

I had heard the finished songs many times. I liked the songs themselves, as well as the mixes. I knew the person responsible for them was exceptionally good at what he did. Yet I had never imagined that some of the sounds I admired had been made in these ways.

Before moving to Canada, I studied music in South Korea.

I spent a considerable amount of time around people who were studying music formally. Many were involved in commercial production, and there were certain approaches to recording and mixing that I encountered repeatedly.

Certain equipment seemed almost standard. Focal monitors and Apollo Twin interfaces were common enough that they did not seem remarkable. There was also a tendency, at least among the people whose work I saw, to follow established production practices quite closely.

These practices existed for good reasons. Reliable monitoring, recording technique and an understanding of signal processing matter. I learned a great deal from studying them, and I still find that knowledge valuable.

But looking through my friend's projects reminded me how easily a familiar method can come to seem like the method one is supposed to use.

He did not seem particularly concerned with whether a solution was conventional. If he wanted a vocal without vibrato, he removed the vibrato. If a sample worked in the song, he used it.

What impressed me was his ability to make a decision, carry it through, and arrive at a result that worked very well. I would not have found the evening nearly as interesting if the projects had simply contained a collection of unusual techniques. There is nothing especially admirable about doing something differently for its own sake.

I told him that I had seen a lot of work by people who studied music at school, and that some of them seemed somewhat conservative in their approach. I wondered aloud whether commercial production might have something to do with it, although I was not sure.

Then I told him what I thought of his work. I liked it because he made decisive choices, and the results were good.

He thanked me, but also mentioned another mixer he knew who worked extensively on commercial music. That mixer, he said, was similarly open to different approaches. He sometimes worked with AirPods and was willing to try different things.

It was a useful counterexample to my speculation. Perhaps the difference I had noticed had less to do with the kind of music they made than with the musicians I happened to interact with.

Still, the contrast was real to me.

I had studied how music was recorded, edited, and mixed. I had also spent years listening to songs, trying to understand what made them sound the way they did.

That evening, I found myself looking at the projects behind songs I already knew. Some of the methods were not what I would have chosen. Some would probably never have occurred to me. But I had heard what they produced before I knew anything about them.

I liked the music first.

Then I saw how he made it.

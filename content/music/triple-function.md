Before entering Howon University, I had already spent a considerable amount of time studying jazz harmony. Modal interchange and minor-key harmony were not particularly difficult for me, and I was comfortable analyzing chord progressions.

During my first year, a professor said something in a harmony class that I found unexpectedly interesting.

Consider the following progression in C major:

`Em7 - A7 - Dm7 - G7`

In conventional jazz harmony, this can be analysed as `IIIm7 - V7/II - IIm7 - V7`. The `Em7` can function as the diatonic `IIIm7` chord while also serving as the related `IIm7` of the following `A7`. This is described as a dual function.

My professor raised a question.

"Among the modes derived from the major scale, aren't there three that can be applied to a minor seventh chord? Dorian, Phrygian, and Aeolian. Then wouldn't it be more appropriate to call it triple function?"

I found it fascinating.

Strictly speaking, the number of available chord scales and the number of harmonic functions are not the same thing. I do not know whether my professor intended the question as a serious alternative to the conventional terminology.

But that was not what interested me.

I had learned to identify harmonic functions and apply chord scales within an established theoretical framework. My professor had taken something familiar and suggested looking at it from a different direction.

At the time, I simply admired the perspective. Afterwards, I began looking more closely at the things harmony theory attempted to describe.

I spent some time going through the material again, reconsidering concepts I had previously accepted without much difficulty. I still liked the way harmony theory approached music. Its classifications were useful, and its explanations often helped me understand relationships I could hear but had not yet identified.

But I sometimes found the grounds for those explanations insufficient. One example was about tritone substitution.

When a dominant seventh chord is replaced by another dominant seventh chord whose root note is a tritone away from the original chord, jazz harmony commonly associates the substitute with the [Lydian dominant scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_scale), also called Lydian flat seven.

I understood the construction of the scale. I understood its relationship to the chord and the voice-leading possibilities it offered. What I wanted to know was why that particular choice had become so common.

In one harmony book, I encountered an explanation that, as I remember it, described the phenomenon like this: musicians seem to have found that choice more natural over a long period of time. I found that answer interesting, but not entirely satisfying.

It explained something about how a musical convention might have become established. It did not fully explain why musicians had come to prefer it in the first place.

There were harmonic relationships that could be described, and there were reasons to find the scale useful. But I wanted to know more about why certain relationships came to sound natural, and why certain possibilities were repeatedly chosen over others.

I gradually came to think that much of what I had learned in harmony theory concerned conventions developed through the choices of musicians over time.

That did not make those conventions arbitrary or useless. They had histories, and there may have been reasons why they developed as they did. Some of those reasons could be explained through acoustics, voice leading, perception, or the relationships within an established musical system.

But identifying a convention was not necessarily the same as explaining its origin.

There was another remark I remembered from a different harmony class.

A professor was showing us an example of harmonic analysis from a jazz standard. During the lesson, the professor recalled an experience from the time he studied music in university.

Some classmates had been discussing how to analyze a particular piece when their professor happened to walk past. Seeing what they were doing, the professor asked whether anyone could really say they were wrong if they analyzed every chord as `I`, or the tonic. My professor told us that the question had been partly a joke, but that there was something important in it.

I agreed. I do not mean that every harmonic analysis is equally convincing.

Given a particular progression, I may find one interpretation much more persuasive than another. It may explain the voice leading, the tonal centre, and the relationships between chords in ways that another interpretation does not. I make those judgments myself.

But finding one analysis more convincing does not necessarily establish that another must be wrong.

Even an interpretation that seems unreasonable within a familiar context may become meaningful under circumstances I have not considered. More fundamentally, I am not sure that harmony theory provides a general criterion by which the truth or falsity of every possible harmonic interpretation can be determined.

That does not prevent me from using it. The purpose of harmonic analysis, as I understand it, is not merely to analyze harmony. It is to help musicians play, describe what happens in music, and understand the relationships they encounter.

For those purposes, an analysis does not necessarily have to be the only possible one. It has to be useful for what I am trying to understand or do.

I can accept that. What I cannot quite do is stop asking why.

I still want to know why certain harmonic relationships sound natural, why particular choices became conventions, and [[Ground and Gravity|what grounds I have for preferring one interpretation over another]].

Perhaps some of those questions belong to fields beyond harmony theory itself.

I think that is what remained with me from my professor's question about triple function.

Not that triple function was necessarily a better name.

But that I could learn an established way of describing music, find it useful, and still ask what justified describing it that way.

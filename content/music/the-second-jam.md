In May 2026, I visited South Korea after completing my studies at Niagara College. I went to Strange Fruit, a small underground live music venue and bar in Hongdae, with two friends from Howon University.

After a band's performance, an open jam began. It would continue until four or five in the morning. I was the first guitarist to play. Some of the musicians working at the venue were seniors from Howon University. I joined them, picked up the guitar, and tried to play.

It did not go well. I struggled to follow the music and could barely find anything worth playing. I knew I was capable of more, but knowing that did not help. The longer I played, the harder it became to recover.

When the jam ended, I went outside and wandered around by myself for quite some time. It was a familiar experience.

On the first day of my first semester at Howon University, I heard a fellow guitarist play *Stella by Starlight*. I knew the tune well. I had practised it extensively and had even performed a prepared solo over it during my entrance exam. But what I heard that day was different.

My classmate was improvising. I could recognize the harmonic ideas and understand the lines he played. There was nothing in his playing that I could not explain theoretically.

What I could not explain was how he made those choices in the moment.

The lines were beautiful, and they seemed to come naturally to him. I remember thinking that he possessed something I could never acquire, however much I practised.

During the first few days, we held a lot of informal jam sessions. I joined them, but hearing the other guitarists had already unsettled me. At one point, we played B-flat rhythm changes at around 160 BPM. It was a progression I knew how to play. Yet I found myself unable to think of anything beyond a few pentatonic phrases.

I had entered university by playing jazz. Yet I was struggling to play something I already knew. Experiences like that kept happening. Gradually, I began avoiding opportunities to play. Eventually, [[I Thought I Was Done With Music|I stopped wanting to listen to music as well]]. For several years, I rarely even carried earbuds when I went out.

There was, however, another experience from that first year.

During the summer break, two instructors at Howon University held a workshop. I had not planned to participate, but a pianist friend asked me to take her place because something had come up.

The piece was Michael Jackson's *Rock with You*. My task was to play the vocal melody on guitar. After hearing my first attempt, one of the instructors told me to try again.

"Concentrate as much as you possibly can. Try as hard as you possibly can to play well. Really concentrate, and really try to do your best."

I did what he asked.

After the second performance, he pointed out how much my playing had changed simply because I had approached it differently.

I remembered that afternoon while wandering outside Strange Fruit. I had come all the way from Canada to Korea. I did not want to leave with that performance as my last experience of playing there.

If I was going to play, I wanted to do it properly. So I went back inside and picked up the guitar again.

This time, the drummer and bassist were members of [THE SOLUTIONS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Solutions), a Korean rock band. We began a one-chord jam in D minor.

The drummer played his first snare hit on beat two.

I cannot describe exactly what was different about that sound. It was not a particular rhythmic figure or some unusual technique. There was nothing complicated about the beat itself.

But the moment I heard it, I felt that he was completely focused on what we were playing.

Completely.

Here was a musician from an established band, usually performing at huge festivals, playing in an underground venue in Hongdae in the early hours of the morning, with someone he did not know. Yet I could feel how seriously he was taking the music.

I found myself concentrating just as intensely. This time, the playing came together.

I remember one phrase.

I bent the thirteenth fret of the B string up a whole tone to D, then moved to the twentieth fret of the high E string and bent up to another D. From there, I moved to the fifth fret of the G string and bent it in the opposite direction to reach D once again.

[[Wrong Notes and Backspace|The phrase had not been prepared]]. I had not planned to stay on the same note for so long, approaching it differently each time. It emerged from the music we were playing together.

As the jam continued, I realized that I was playing quite well. Not exceptionally, perhaps, but well enough that I was satisfied with what I was contributing.

I stayed at Strange Fruit until shortly before sunrise, listening to other musicians and joining several more jams. I was satisfied with all of my subsequent performances.

Before the drummer left, I said, "Thank you."

I do not think that night changed how I evaluate myself as a guitarist. I remain aware of the considerable distance between my playing and the standard I associate with professional musicians. Nor do I think that concentrating guarantees a good performance.

But I remember what the instructor told me, and I remember that first snare hit. In both cases, something about the way I approached the music changed what I was able to play.

There is another part of that night that has become more important to me with time.

While I was playing, I was mostly pleased that I had managed to produce some decent phrases. Looking back, however, I feel more grateful for the opportunity to have played with them at all.

I have come to think that, for musicians, there may be no greater expression of respect than taking the music they are making together seriously. I do not know what that drummer thought of my playing. I do not know whether he remembers the jam.

What I remember is that he played his first snare hit, I heard someone giving the music his full attention, and I wanted to give it mine.

For a few minutes, we played together.

I am glad I went back inside.

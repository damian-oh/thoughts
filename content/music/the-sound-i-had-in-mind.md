A songwriter friend of mine in South Korea asked me to record guitar for [a song](https://youtu.be/Y4G6bDkNt08) he was working on.

He told me that it was a song that needed a Gibson ES-335. I had been playing an ES-335 for ten years. I thought I had a fairly good idea of what he meant.

For the intro, I followed the piano melody using octaves, played with the thumb of my right hand. There was also a four-bar guitar solo in the middle of the song. I played along with the track a few times, trying different things.

Eventually, I started the solo with a line in the style of [George Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Benson). It was a kind of phrase I had played many times before, and it fit the song well. After that, I simply tried to make a good melody. The rest came surprisingly easily. I finished the solo with a double-stop line that I might even describe as somewhat clichéd.

I played through the phrases a few times, found the ones I liked, and recorded them again. My friend was happy with the results.

There was nothing particularly complicated about the process. But there is something about it that I find difficult to explain.

[[What Is Not Made Explicit|As I have described before]], I cannot reproduce sounds in my mind. I do not hear a melody internally when I think about it. I can remember that I have heard something, and I can remember information about it, but I cannot recreate the experience of hearing it.

Yet I have been playing guitar for many years, and I can still make musical decisions. When my friend told me that the song needed an ES-335, I understood something about what he wanted. I knew the instrument, the kinds of sounds I could produce with it, and the ways I might approach the arrangement.

But I did not hear those sounds in my head.

The same was true of the guitar solo. I knew how to play certain phrases. I knew the kinds of lines I associated with George Benson. I could decide to try one of them, play it, and hear whether it worked. Once I found something I liked, I could play it again.

I find it difficult to explain exactly what happens when I remember a phrase well enough to reproduce it. Sometimes I can describe the sequence of notes and fingerings explicitly. I know which strings and frets to use, and in what order. At other times, playing feels more like a physical activity that I have practised repeatedly.

Most of the time, I cannot clearly separate the two. I seem to rely on both. Nor do I know precisely how I chose each note in the solo. I was listening to the track, playing, and making decisions along the way. I wanted the melody to work, but I did not know in advance exactly what that melody would be.

I do not think this necessarily makes my way of writing music unusual. Other musicians may also discover melodies by playing them rather than imagining them first. What I find interesting is the distinction between knowing what I want to attempt and being able to experience the result before attempting it.

I could understand what my friend meant by asking for an ES-335. I could draw on phrases I had learned, choose how to begin a solo, and recognize when something sounded right.

But none of that required me to hear the finished part before I played it. When I first played along with the track, I did not know what those four bars would sound like.

I knew what I could try. When I heard something I liked, I played it again.

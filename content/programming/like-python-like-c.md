I first learned what an HTML `<h1>` tag was after entering Niagara College. One of my classmates, meanwhile, had been programming since he was quite young.

During our final semester, we ended up working together on a capstone project. The course involved developing software for an actual local business, based on its requirements. Our team was assigned to build an application for a physiotherapy and Pilates studio.

Over the course of the project, I noticed something about the way my classmate and I approached programming. We were quite different.

When I encountered a problem, I generally wanted to find a solution that worked, make sure it satisfied the requirements, and move on. There was a deadline, and the project involved more than writing code. We had documentation to complete, presentations to prepare, and other responsibilities that demanded our attention.

My classmate approached problems differently.

Even when the code was already working, he would continue examining it. Was there a safer way to implement the same behaviour? What problems might arise under conditions we had not considered? Could the structure be improved so that the code explained itself?

He seemed unwilling to leave a problem alone merely because it had been solved. I admired that. Sometimes, watching him work, I thought, "That is what a real developer looks like."

It was not that I thought his approach was always more appropriate. I had reasons for working the way I did. A working feature was not the only thing the project required, and time spent improving one part of the application was time unavailable for something else.

Still, there was something impressive about his willingness to examine a problem long after I would have considered it finished.

He did not seem satisfied with knowing that something worked. He wanted to understand why it worked, what might cause it to fail, and whether it could be made more reliable.

I remember one occasion particularly well.

[Cursor](https://cursor.com/) had released Composer, and my classmate wanted to try it. In less than twenty-four hours, he opened three pull requests containing dozens of commits.

I reviewed them. There was nothing I wanted to change. I merged all three.

The project continued, and we eventually completed it without any major conflict between us.

Between the two of us, we had written most of the code. I also handled the presentation and documentation.

Looking back, I do not think our different approaches were necessarily incompatible. We were working towards the same goal, but we seemed to have different ideas about when a particular piece of work was finished.

For me, meeting the requirements was usually a good reason to move on, provided I had no specific reason to believe something was wrong.

For him, the fact that the code worked did not necessarily mean that there was nothing more worth understanding.

I do not think either position can be reduced to a simple rule about how software should be developed. There are situations in which moving on too quickly creates problems that become expensive to correct. There are also situations in which continuing to refine an implementation prevents more important work from being completed.

I cannot say that my decisions were always better than his, or that his were always better than mine. But I can say that I respected the way he worked.

Some time after graduation, I told him that I had noticed a difference between us. I said that I coded like Python, while he coded like C.

It was not a technical comparison of the two languages. I was trying to describe something about our attitudes towards programming.

I was comfortable relying on an [[What I Do Not Need to Know|abstraction]] once it did what I needed. He seemed less willing to consider something finished because it worked, continuing to question its assumptions and look for problems that had not yet appeared.

He replied, "I will take that as a compliment, because I like C."

During the same conversation, he also said, "You are better than me."

I do not know exactly what he meant.

Perhaps he thought I was a better programmer. Perhaps he valued some aspect of my approach that I had not considered particularly remarkable. Perhaps he was simply being generous.

Whatever he meant, I knew that I admired him.

Yet if I could return to that final semester and choose how to work again, I think I would make the same choices.

I would not want to become the kind of developer he was.

But I would still look at him and think, "That is what a real developer looks like."

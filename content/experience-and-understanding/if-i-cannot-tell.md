I sometimes say that if I could not tell Coke and Sprite apart with my eyes closed, I would just drink whichever one was in front of me.

It is a joke, of course, but it shows how I feel about blind tests. When a distinction is supposed to be about how something tastes or sounds, I do not think it means much to me if I cannot perceive it without knowing what I am tasting or hearing beforehand.

I am reminded of this whenever I watch blind test videos on YouTube. Whether the comparison is between instruments, gear, or audio formats, I never feel confident that I could tell them apart.

Consider the difference between rosewood and maple fretboards. How they feel under the fingers is a different matter, and a guitarist might prefer one over the other. Identifying the material by sound alone, however, seems very difficult.

The same question comes up with audio specifications, such as differences in bit depth. Higher bit depths have clear technical uses during recording and mixing. But when it comes to listening to a finished track, I doubt that I could hear the difference between properly prepared 16-bit and 24-bit versions.

A difference can be real and measurable. If the claim is about sound, however, and I cannot tell the difference in a blind test, it is hard for me to treat it as important.

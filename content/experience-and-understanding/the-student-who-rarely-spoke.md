I worked as a guitar instructor at a music academy in South Korea for about a year and a half. Some of my students were in their early teens. I taught them how to play guitar, read music, understand harmony, and develop their technique.

One day, I was assigned a new student who was in her first year of middle school, roughly Grade 7 in Canada. She was rather quiet, but she really wanted to learn guitar.

I noticed how little she spoke during our first lesson. I did not see much reason to make her behave otherwise. If she was quiet, she was quiet. After the lesson, the academy director told me that the student had gone home and proudly told her parents about it.

I was relieved to hear that.

We continued having lessons for several months. She remained a quiet student. I would ask her questions, and sometimes there would be a pause before she answered. I did not mind. We could still communicate and continue with the lesson.

I remember one lesson with her particularly well. She told me how much she loved playing guitar. I told her that she could be a great guitarist. Something about her reminded me of myself when I first started playing guitar.

Eventually, I had to leave my job because I was moving to Canada.

The director told me that the student was very disappointed, although she had not shown it to me. So, the director deliberately kept her assigned to me until my last day at the academy.

I had taught many students, but she is the one I remember most.

There was something about teaching that I had begun to think about. I could decide what knowledge or technique I wanted to teach. I could prepare exercises, explain musical concepts, and correct mistakes. But that did not mean those were the only things a student might take from the lessons.

I began to think that teaching someone was not something I should take lightly.

After completing my first year of studies in Canada, I returned to South Korea for a break. I visited the academy where I had worked and met the director again. She told me that the student had experienced some friction with the instructor who came after me, and that she had eventually stopped attending the academy.

I did not know the details of what had happened between them. Then the director asked me something.

"She was rather quiet, wasn't she?"

I told her that she was.

"But if I asked her something and waited a little, she would answer. Things like 'I like it,' 'No,' or 'Yes.'"

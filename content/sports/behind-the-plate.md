I was better at football than baseball, but baseball was my favourite sport.

I played catcher.

There was something I liked about being the only player on defence facing the other direction. While the rest of the field looked towards home plate, I looked out at them. I could see the pitcher, the infielders, and the outfielders from behind the batter.

But what I enjoyed most was the responsibility that came with the position.

A catcher cannot afford to lose track of the ball. A missed pitch can allow runners to advance, sometimes turning an ordinary situation into a serious problem. There are also responsibilities beyond catching. The catcher communicates the number of outs, adjusts defensive positioning, receives signs from the coaches, and visits the mound when the pitcher needs help.

I liked being responsible for those things. I liked directing the defence while knowing that a mistake of my own could have consequences.

I remember one school baseball tournament particularly well.

In the first game, I came to the plate for my first at-bat. The opposing pitcher threw an ordinary fastball. I was a right-handed thrower and a left-handed batter, and I took the ball the other way, driving it into the gap in left-centre field.

It hit the fence.

I ran towards first and continued to second. As I approached second base, I noticed that a runner ahead of me had reached third but was hesitating over whether to go home.

I pointed towards home plate and kept running. He went home, and I reached third safely.

It was a two-run triple.

I cannot remember many details about the rest of that play, but I remember the runner hesitating and my pointing towards home. It was a good way to begin a tournament.

Later in the same game, I had quite a different experience behind the plate.

One of our pitchers was a student a year above me who had previously played elite-level baseball. By the time we played together, he had stopped playing at that level.

He was having a terrible time finding the strike zone.

He walked one batter, then another. His control did not seem to improve. Eventually, I went to the mound with our coach to talk to him and try to settle him down.

It did not work.

He continued to struggle with his control and was eventually taken out of the game.

I remember that situation because there was very little I could do once he could no longer throw strikes consistently. I could receive the pitches, communicate with him, and try to help him settle down. But I could not throw the ball for him.

The tournament continued. After that first game, something began to change in my batting. I began swinging harder and taking a larger, more upward swing, apparently trying to send the ball farther.

I noticed what I was doing. My coach noticed it too.

He told me that I was trying to hit the ball too far with an uppercut swing, and that I should instead bring the bat through the path of the incoming pitch.

I understood what he meant. I had already noticed the problem myself. But understanding it did not seem to help very much.

I tried to correct my swing. I wanted to return to the way I had been hitting before the triple, but when I stepped into the batter's box, the same tendency kept returning.

I began striking out more often, and more of my at-bats ended in outs. I knew what I was doing wrong, or at least what I needed to change, but I could not consistently make my body do it.

We failed to advance to the knockout stage.

I still remember the triple. I also remember standing behind the plate while an older pitcher struggled to throw strikes, and standing in the batter's box during the following games, trying to correct a swing that had become too large.

I knew what my coach wanted me to do. I wanted to do it too.

But I kept swinging the way I had been swinging.

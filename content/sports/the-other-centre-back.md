When I played football in school, I was a centre-back.

I relied heavily on my speed and physical strength. I was faster than most of the players I faced, and I knew I could run down any attacker breaking through our defensive line, even if I started sprinting three steps behind.

I played with a high line. I told the other defenders to push forward, always insisting that no one should stand behind me. A single defender staying too deep could keep the entire opposition attack onside, and I preferred to defend the space behind us myself.

There was something I particularly enjoyed about that space.

An opposing striker would make a run behind our line, expecting to reach a through ball. I would turn, accelerate, and catch him before he could do much with it. Then came the physical contest. I would use my body to force him away from the ball, leaving him on the ground, before clearing it or passing it to my fellow centre-back.

I liked continuing with the next play as though nothing remarkable had happened.

What I enjoyed even more was watching what happened afterward. Sometimes, after a few such encounters, the opposing team would become less willing to play balls into the space behind us.

That was satisfying. I liked knowing that my presence had become something they had to account for.

But I did not play alone.

My partner at centre-back was a very different kind of defender.

He had extraordinary endurance, even outperforming elite track athletes in an 800-metre race. But what made him such a good defender was not merely his physical ability.

He was calm.

I cannot remember him losing his temper during a match. I cannot remember him making the kinds of careless mistakes that came from rushing a decision or becoming frustrated. Whatever happened around him, he seemed to remain the same.

For someone who played the way I did, he was an ideal partner.

I could be aggressive in my positioning, challenge strikers directly, and try to control the defensive line. Beside me was someone I could rely on to make sensible decisions even under pressure.

Our differences were especially apparent at set pieces.

Whenever we had a corner or a free kick that offered a chance to attack, I went forward. I competed for headers in the opposition penalty area. I was comfortable leaving my usual position because I trusted my speed and recovery. If possession changed and the opposition counterattacked, I believed I could get back.

But there was another reason I felt comfortable going forward.

He stayed behind.

Every time I went up for a set piece, he remained in defence. I did not have to wonder whether he would follow me into the penalty area or leave the space behind us unprotected. I knew where he would be.

It is easy for me to remember the moments when I caught a striker, won a physical contest, or stopped an attack. Those were the parts of defending I enjoyed most, and they were immediately visible.

His contribution was quieter.

He did not need to make every tackle or chase every striker. His positioning, consistency, and composure allowed the rest of us to play with greater confidence. At least, that was how I experienced playing beside him.

I do not remember us having elaborate conversations about our respective roles. What I remember is how naturally they seemed to fit together.

I would go forward for the header.

He would stay behind.

I have not spoken to him since we left school. I do not quite know why. As far as I know, he has since become a firefighter.

I still think he was the best defensive partner I ever had.

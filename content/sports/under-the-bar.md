I started powerlifting because I liked squatting.

There was something enjoyable about putting a heavy barbell on my back, lowering myself under its weight, and standing up again. I did not need much more of a reason than that. At one point, I became capable of squatting 530 lb (240 kg) with nothing more than a lifting belt. I did not use knee sleeves or wraps.

I remember the feeling of unracking a heavy barbell. The moment I stepped away from the rack, I could feel how much weight I had placed on my shoulders. Sometimes, the sensation was almost frightening. It felt as though a mistake could have fatal consequences.

I knew there were safety bars to catch the weight if I failed. I was not particularly interested in putting myself in actual danger.

What I liked was the concentration. There was a heavy barbell on my back, and I had to stand up with it. The weight demanded my attention in a way that very few other things did.

I would lower myself until my hip crease was below the top of my knees, then drive back up.

At sufficiently heavy weights, the effort could feel almost unbearable. I felt like my entire body was tearing apart. There was little room for anything else. Then, sometimes, I stood up.

What I enjoyed most was not the relief of having finished. It was the satisfaction of having done it, followed by the realization of how completely I had been concentrating. Often, my next thought was that I wanted to try something heavier.

I liked deadlifting too.

In fact, there was something about preparing for a heavy deadlift that I enjoyed even more than preparing for a squat.

I use long, straight lifting straps. They take more effort to wrap around the bar than some other designs, but that is why I prefer them. I like the feeling of wrapping the straps around the barbell, tightening them, and feeling my hands become firmly connected to it.

It makes the task ahead feel more immediate. I am going to lift that barbell.

The process of securing my grip seems to increase my concentration. By the time I am ready to pull, I have already spent some time preparing myself for that particular weight.

Bench pressing is another matter.

I have always been weak at it. Even when I could squat 530 lb, my best bench press was only 225 lb (102 kg). I did not enjoy it as much as squatting or deadlifting.

There was no particular principle behind that preference. I simply liked some lifts more than others.

I also liked discovering where my limits were.

When I failed a squat, I usually wanted to try the same weight once more. If I had successfully squatted 530 lb and then failed at 540 or 550, I did not regard the first failure as a final answer.

I wanted to find out whether I could do it, so I tried again. Sometimes I failed a second time.

Failure did not usually leave me feeling discouraged. I even found something satisfying about discovering that a weight was beyond what I could lift at that moment.

Of course, a failed lift does not establish an exact limit. Fatigue, technique, and many other factors affect what happens under the bar. Nor does wanting another attempt necessarily mean that making one is sensible.

Still, I liked finding out.

The numbers mattered to me. I cannot pretend that successfully lifting a weight below my previous best would have given me the same satisfaction as setting a new record.

But the numbers were not the reason I began. I liked squatting before I had any impressive numbers to associate with it. As the weights increased, the thing I liked about it remained much the same.

The feeling of taking the barbell out of the rack. The weight settling on my shoulders. The effort of lowering myself and standing up again. After a successful lift, the moment when I realized how completely I had given my attention to that single task.

I would look at the weight I had just lifted.

Then I would wonder whether I could lift a little more.

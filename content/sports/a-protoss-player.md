I first encountered [StarCraft](https://starcraft.blizzard.com/en-us) when I was about ten or eleven.

I was visiting a friend's house when I saw his older brother playing it. I did not understand much of what was happening, but I found it interesting enough to ask my father to teach me.

My father had played StarCraft quite seriously throughout the 2000s. Although he no longer played as frequently, he still returned to it occasionally.

He started with the [[Ground and Gravity|fundamentals]].

He explained what minerals and gas were, how supply worked, and why I should never stop producing workers in the early stages of a game. He taught me the basic build orders and unit compositions for all three races: Terran, Zerg, and Protoss.

I practised all three.

I do not quite remember why I eventually settled on Protoss. There was nothing in particular that I disliked about the other races. At some point, I simply found myself playing Protoss more often, until Terran and Zerg became increasingly unfamiliar to me.

I became a Protoss player.

My preferred approach was straightforward. I liked building a large ground army, particularly Zealots and Dragoons, by adding Pylons at the right moments and keeping my Gateways working. I wanted to produce as many units as possible and overwhelm the opponent with numbers.

Of the professional players I watched, Do Jae-wook probably had the style most similar to mine, although he was not necessarily my favourite.

I admired players such as Park Jung-suk, Kim Taek-yong, and Heo Young-moo.

One match in particular has stayed with me.

In the fourth game of the 2012 StarLeague semi-final against Kim Myung-woon, Heo Young-moo was in a terrible position.

His natural expansion had fallen to a Hydralisk rush. A subsequent Lurker drop had killed a considerable number of workers in his main base. His economy was severely damaged, and there seemed to be very little he could do to recover.

The game looked over.

It was the final StarLeague, and Heo was one game away from elimination. At that moment, he was the last Protoss player left in StarLeague history. He could have surrendered, and I doubt many people would have questioned the decision.

But he did not.

With what little he had left, he gathered an army of Zealots, Dragoons, High Templars, and Archons. There seemed to be no realistic prospect of rebuilding his economy and returning to a normal game. He had one remaining opportunity, and he committed everything to it.

The army moved out.

I remember watching those Protoss units advance, knowing how little remained behind them. It reminded me of Zeratul leading the last surviving Protoss into a battle they could scarcely hope to win.

Somehow, Heo broke through. He won the game.

I found it genuinely moving. I had watched plenty of professional StarCraft, including matches that were probably more technically impressive. But something about that final attack stayed with me.

I was a Protoss player, after all.

Years later, StarCraft: Remastered was released, and I returned to the game for a while. I played ranked matches, usually hovering around 1,500 rating points. I was not particularly good. Even staying in the upper half of the ladder was not easy.

Still, I knew what kind of player I wanted to be.

In a Protoss mirror match at that level, I was confident that I could produce more Dragoons than my opponent. I had spent enough time practising build orders and production cycles to feel comfortable with that part of the game.

Controlling the army was another matter.

Once both players approached the 200-supply limit, I often struggled to manage large engagements effectively. Terran was particularly difficult to play against. Producing a large army was one thing. Using it well against Siege Tanks and Vultures was something else entirely.

A few months after Remastered came out, my father mentioned that he had heard about the release. He suggested that we go to a [PC bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC_bang) and play together.

I chose Protoss, as always. My father decided to play all three races against me.

He started with Terran.

I managed to win by loading Zealots into a Shuttle and dropping them directly beside his Siege Tanks. It was a way of getting past the tanks' long-range fire and forcing them into close combat.

Next, he chose Protoss.

I was not confident that I could beat him in a conventional game, so I decided to use a 9/9 Gateway rush, building two Gateways early and attacking with Zealots before he could establish his economy.

It worked. I was fortunate to win that game.

His final race was Zerg.

This was the closest game of the three. He opened with a Zergling rush, and I had a hard time defending it. At several points, I thought I was about to lose.

Eventually, I managed to drop Dark Templars at one of his outer expansions with a Shuttle. They killed more Drones than I had expected, damaging his economy enough for me to recover.

I narrowly won.

What surprised me was how close the games had been.

I had spent months practising different build orders, learning how to execute them, and playing against people who were actively trying to improve. My father, meanwhile, had barely played StarCraft for almost ten years.

Yet I had struggled against him in all three matchups.

I no longer play StarCraft, and I do not follow professional matches either.

But I still have a particular appreciation for the game. I have heard people say that [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_(game)) is difficult to learn, but that its appeal becomes much clearer once one develops a sufficient understanding of it.

StarCraft, like baseball, has always felt somewhat like that to me.

I remember watching Heo Young-moo gather the last of his Protoss army and send it into what looked like a hopeless battle.

I also remember sitting across from my [[What I Hope to Be Able to Say#^father|father]] at a PC bang, trying to find a way to beat someone who had taught me, years earlier, why I should never stop producing workers.

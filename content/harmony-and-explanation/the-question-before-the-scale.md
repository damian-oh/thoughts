I have studied jazz harmony for many years. During that time, I learned various approaches to choosing scales for improvisation over chord progressions.

One familiar convention is to use the Lydian dominant scale over a tritone substitution. For example, in a `Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7` progression in C major, the `G7` chord can be replaced by `Db7`. A commonly recommended scale over `Db7` is Db Lydian dominant, the fourth mode of Ab melodic minor.

I knew this association, but I became curious about why that particular scale was considered appropriate. I could explain how the scale was constructed. I could identify the notes it contained and describe their intervals relative to the chord. But those descriptions did not explain why I should choose it.

I began with a question:

*Can the application of the Lydian dominant scale to tritone substitutions be derived from more fundamental principles of tonal harmony, without presupposing the melodic minor scale or existing chord-scale conventions?*

The distinction mattered to me. If I began by assuming that `Db7` should take Db Lydian dominant because it is the fourth mode of Ab melodic minor, I would already be relying on the convention I wanted to investigate.

Knowing where a scale comes from does not, by itself, establish why that scale is suitable for a given harmonic situation. I wanted to know whether the association could be explained without taking it as a starting assumption.

But the more I considered the question, the more I noticed that it contained another assumption. I was asking why one scale was suitable without first establishing what suitability meant.

A pitch might be considered suitable because it belongs to the chord, because it creates a particular tension, because it leads to a note in the following chord, or because it produces a sound that a musician wants in that context.

These possibilities need not agree. A note that produces strong dissonance against a chord might still serve a musical purpose. A note that belongs to a familiar scale might be less effective in a particular melodic phrase.

Nor was it clear that the suitability of individual pitches would be sufficient to determine the suitability of an entire pitch collection. A collection contains possibilities. It does not specify which notes will be played, in what order, for how long, or with what rhythmic emphasis.

I therefore revised the question.

*What makes a pitch collection suitable for a chord within a given harmonic context?*

This question is broader than the one I started with. It does not assume that there is a single correct scale for every chord, or that an existing chord-scale convention must be derivable from a more fundamental theory. It also leaves open what kind of explanation would count as successful.

A theoretical explanation might account for relationships among pitches, chord tones, and voice leading. But whether those relationships are sufficient to explain musical suitability remains to be investigated.

I want to examine proposed explanations, identify their assumptions, and consider cases in which they might fail. I also want to distinguish between explaining why a choice is possible, why it is commonly used, and why it might be preferable to another choice. Those are not the same questions.

Before asking why a particular scale belongs over a chord, I want to understand what it would mean for a scale to belong there at all.

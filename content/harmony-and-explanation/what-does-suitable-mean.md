I began this investigation by asking what makes a pitch collection suitable for a chord within a given harmonic context.

I discussed the question with ChatGPT. We considered several possible criteria, including consonance, harmonic stability, voice leading, harmonic function, and expressive possibilities. None of these seemed sufficient on its own.

For example, a pitch might create strong dissonance against a chord but still serve a musical purpose. A collection might contain the chord tones without necessarily allowing a musician to produce a satisfying melodic phrase.

I then raised a question about a more extreme case.

Suppose a musician deliberately uses C Locrian over a `Cmaj7` chord. If the musician intends to produce dissonance and achieves the desired musical effect, can C Locrian be considered suitable for that chord?

ChatGPT suggested distinguishing the suitability of a pitch collection from the suitability of a musical application. Even if an improvisation achieved its intended effect, that would not necessarily establish that the pitch collection itself was suitable in a more general sense.

To explore the distinction, I decided to improvise over `Cmaj7` using C Locrian and observe what happened. I also played over the same chord using C Lydian.

The two collections differ considerably in their relationship to `Cmaj7`.

C Lydian contains C, D, E, F♯, G, A, and B. All four notes of `Cmaj7` belong to the collection.

C Locrian contains C, D♭, E♭, F, G♭, A♭, and B♭. Of the four chord tones, only C belongs to the collection.

I was not satisfied with either improvisation, but I did not attribute that dissatisfaction to the scales themselves. What interested me was how differently I experienced playing them.

With C Lydian, I could concentrate on developing melodic lines without paying much conscious attention to dissonance against the underlying chord.

C Locrian was different. I could hear the dissonances against `Cmaj7`. Initially, they felt like obstacles.

I felt that the dissonances were imposing a direction on my playing. I thought that if I kept paying attention to them, I might struggle to develop the improvisation into something I found meaningful. So I tried to ignore the vertical relationship between the notes and the chord, concentrating instead on the horizontal development of the melody.

After a while, the dissonances became less prominent in my attention. I grew somewhat more comfortable with the sound.

I told ChatGPT about these experiences. I found that I could develop melodic ideas using either collection, but I did not consider them equally suitable. C Lydian had allowed me to play without consciously managing its relationship to the underlying harmony, whereas C Locrian initially demanded that attention.

As we discussed the difference, ChatGPT introduced the idea of *melodic freedom*. It concerned the freedom a musician experiences when developing melodic ideas without conflicting demands from the underlying harmony.

ChatGPT formulated a provisional hypothesis:

*A pitch collection's suitability within a harmonic context is partly determined by the extent to which it permits horizontal melodic development without imposing conflicting vertical harmonic demands.*

I found the idea worth investigating, but I was not convinced that fewer constraints necessarily meant greater musical freedom. A restriction might obstruct an idea I wanted to develop. But it might also lead me towards possibilities I would not otherwise have considered.

Nor had the experiment established that melodic freedom determines suitability. I had played different improvisations with different pitch collections. I had not controlled their melodic contours, rhythms, phrasing, or other musical decisions. My growing comfort with C Locrian might also have reflected familiarity with the sound rather than any change in its harmonic suitability.

Although I could use C Locrian to develop melodic ideas over `Cmaj7`, I would still choose C Lydian in that context. I suspected that the dissonances themselves were part of what made C Locrian less desirable to me. But I was not sure how to explain that preference.

The experiment had not established a criterion for suitability. What it gave me was an experience to examine, along with a hypothesis proposed during the discussion.

I want to investigate whether melodic freedom can be defined more precisely, how it relates to harmonic consonance, and whether it provides a useful explanation of musical suitability.

I know which collection I would choose. I am less certain what that choice establishes.
